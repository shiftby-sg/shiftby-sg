# Purpose and definition

Human–AI Accountability supports responsible, informed, traceable human and organizational answerability when AI creates, transforms, recommends, evaluates, or uses information and decisions.

**Definition:** Human–AI Accountability is the organizational capability to assign, preserve, exercise, evidence, review, challenge, and adapt explicit human and organizational responsibility and authority throughout AI-enabled work, across people, systems, suppliers, decisions, policies, controls, lifecycle changes, incidents, and outcomes.

Its primary purpose is to sustain human and organizational answerability for AI-enabled work through explicit responsibility and legitimate authority that are exercised and evidenced throughout that work.

# Enterprise need

Organizations must preserve responsibility, authority, evidence, and answerability across people, systems, suppliers, and organizational boundaries. This need exists without AI, but human-only approaches can become costly, slow, fragmented, and difficult to sustain at enterprise scale. AI makes it especially acute by influencing understanding, recommendations, decisions, and execution.

Human–AI Accountability ensures that AI participation does not obscure who had authority, who remained responsible, what was reviewed, what evidence supported action, which policies and controls applied, whether oversight was meaningful, and how intervention, correction, escalation, or remedy could occur.

Without this capability, organizations may mistake AI output for authority; confuse participation, automation, delegation, approval, and authorization; leave ownership and oversight nominal or fragmented; or lose evidence, accountability continuity, and the ability to reconstruct, challenge, intervene, correct, or remedy consequential activity.

# Accountability, authority, and oversight

The capability preserves these distinctions in context without imposing one universal operating model:

- **Responsibility:** the duty to perform work, exercise care, review information, or take action.

- **Accountability:** the obligation to answer for whether responsibilities and authority were exercised appropriately and for resulting consequences.

- **Authority:** the legitimate power to decide, approve, permit, restrict, intervene, suspend, or accept risk.

- **Ownership:** continuing stewardship of a business outcome, capability, process, system, control, or risk.

- **Delegation:** assignment of work or bounded decision rights without silently transferring ultimate organizational accountability.

- **Approval:** an explicit decision to permit or accept something within defined authority and conditions.

- **Oversight:** informed supervision, challenge, monitoring, and intervention appropriate to consequence.

- **Assurance:** evidence-based evaluation of whether responsibilities, controls, and accountability conditions are designed and operating as intended.

- **Liability:** a related legal concept whose determination depends on applicable law and is outside this capability definition.

## Human accountability and AI participation

People and organizations remain accountable for AI-enabled work: defining purpose, ownership, legitimate authority, review and evidence requirements, acceptable reliance, risk acceptance, intervention, escalation, correction, and remedy. AI may propose, recommend, support review and evidence assembly, and execute within organizationally established bounds. Legitimate human or organizational authority remains required to decide or authorize where a decision right applies and to accept continued reliance or residual risk. Review, challenge, override, suspension, escalation, and re-authorization rights must be assigned proportionately to consequence and risk. Accountability conditions remain proportionate to purpose, consequence, uncertainty, and risk.

AI is not the source, owner, final approver, independent confirmer, or bearer of organizational accountability. Participation and capability do not confer authority or authorization. Agent identity, authentication, technical permissions, access controls, policy-engine decisions, and ability to act may evidence how execution was enabled or constrained, but do not by themselves establish legitimate organizational authority, risk-acceptance rights, or accountability. AI must not independently acquire or assume authority for consequential decisions; bounded execution requires authority, conditions, oversight, and intervention rights established by the responsible organization.

Where AI tasks or agents retrieve enterprise information, use tools or pass work between systems, protection responsibilities remain explicit. Human–AI Accountability identifies who owns the requirements for protecting information and instructions and controlling access; who operates and reviews the relevant controls; what evidence demonstrates their operation; and who may accept or escalate exceptions. These responsibilities remain identifiable across internal teams and suppliers.

AI may improve coverage, timeliness, consistency, and scale by detecting accountability gaps, linking evidence and actions, monitoring conditions, reconstructing participation and transformations, and surfacing uncertainty and unresolved issues. These benefits remain subject to human and organizational responsibility, review, challenge, correction, and escalation.

Source-grounded evidence, human interpretation, retrospective inference, and AI-generated content are distinct states. AI-generated explanations, records, scores, or recommendations must not be treated as source evidence or authority merely because they are coherent or technically produced.

## Meaningful human oversight

Human oversight is meaningful only where the responsible person or body has, proportionate to consequence:

- appropriate competence and role clarity;

- access to sufficient information, evidence, limitations, and uncertainty;

- adequate time and organizational support;

- legitimate authority to question, reject, suspend, override, or escalate;

- practical ability to intervene before or after consequential action;

- freedom from automatic, coerced, or performative approval;

- clear escalation and fallback paths;

- review evidence sufficient to demonstrate that oversight occurred.

The capability does not require a human to approve every AI-supported action. It requires explicit, risk- and consequence-appropriate accountability conditions and prevents nominal “human-in-the-loop” design from being treated as accountability by default.

# Accountability lifecycle

Human–AI Accountability applies throughout AI-enabled work, not only at approval or deployment:

1. **Opportunity and use-case selection:** define purpose, intended outcomes, affected stakeholders, accountable owner, permitted use, and unacceptable use.

2. **Design, acquisition, or sourcing:** assign responsibility for requirements, supplier evaluation, risk acceptance, design choices, and contractual accountability.

3. **Data and knowledge preparation:** establish ownership, provenance expectations, permissible use, quality responsibilities, and treatment of gaps or bias.

4. **Testing and validation:** define who validates, what evidence is sufficient, which limitations remain, and who may accept residual risk.

5. **Deployment and authorization:** record authority, permitted boundaries, review conditions, intervention rights, and reliance constraints.

6. **Operational use and reliance:** preserve who used, reviewed, accepted, rejected, or overrode AI-supported work and under what conditions.

7. **Monitoring and change:** reassess accountability when changes to models, prompts, data, configurations, suppliers, policies, controls, roles, permissions, risk appetite, reliance conditions, or operating conditions materially affect previously valid responsibility, authority, oversight, control, evidence, or reliance assumptions. A material-change trigger creates a need for review or revalidation; it does not itself authorize continuation, revocation, suspension, risk acceptance, or re-authorization. The appropriate response remains a decision of the responsible human or organizational authority.

8. **Incident response and remediation:** identify accountable responders, investigate failure, contain harm, correct downstream effects, notify, escalate, and remedy where appropriate.

9. **Retirement or replacement:** preserve decisions, responsibilities, retained evidence, unresolved obligations, and continuity during transition.

# Evidence and assurance

Accountability must be evidence-backed and testable, with unresolved, conflicting, missing, expired, or weak information visible. Preserve source awareness, transformations, omissions, and uncertainty, using contemporaneous evidence where available and clearly labelled retrospective reconstruction where necessary. The capability enables the organization to define and preserve:

- evidence required for authorization, reliance, review, monitoring, exceptions, and incidents;

- evidence provenance, status, quality, recency, completeness, and uncertainty;

- accountable evidence owners and reviewers;

- criteria for evidence sufficiency and acceptance;

- evidence that controls operated, not merely that controls existed;

- exceptions, waivers, control failures, and compensating actions;

- assurance conclusions, challenge, disagreement, and residual uncertainty;

- independent review where consequence, conflict, regulation, or risk makes it proportionate;

- the distinction between contemporaneous evidence and retrospective inference.

Technical logs and AI observability can support investigation through evidence of system behaviour, model and prompt changes, inputs and outputs, retrieval context, interventions, failures, latency, uncertainty signals, and downstream effects. They do not by themselves establish truth, correctness, authorization, legitimate authority, meaningful oversight, sufficient evidence, exercised responsibility, compliance, fairness, or accountability. Telemetry remains distinct from accountable ownership, approved authority, decision rationale, human review, and remedy records; interpretations require evidence review and authorized human judgment.

Evidence that Human–AI Accountability exists and operates should distinguish between defined accountability conditions, evidence that those conditions were actually exercised in consequential work, and evidence of sustained adoption or continuity over time. Downstream outcomes such as improved trust, decision quality, resilience, transformation success, speed, scale, or reduced incidents may be relevant effects or context, but they do not independently prove that the capability exists or is effective.

# Policy, regulatory, and framework alignment

Human–AI Accountability does not create or replace every internal policy, law, standard, regulatory interpretation, or compliance framework. It enables an organization to operationalize and answer for applicable requirements by:

- identifying relevant policies, standards, contractual duties, and external obligations;

- translating them into accountable roles, decision rights, review conditions, controls, and evidence expectations;

- linking requirements and controls to accountable owners;

- preserving interpretations, applicability decisions, approved exceptions, and conflicts;

- showing which requirements and versions applied at a particular time;

- identifying coverage gaps or inconsistent requirements;

- reassessing accountability when policies, standards, regulations, or circumstances change;

- demonstrating whether required responsibilities and controls were exercised.

External references are alignment and validation lenses, not sources of ShiftBy authority:

- [NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) — risk-governance outcomes, accountable roles, lifecycle management, measurement, and monitoring.

- [NIST AI 600-1, Generative AI Profile](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1) — generative-AI-specific risks, governance actions, evidence, incident considerations, and third-party dependencies.

- [ISO/IEC 42001](https://www.iso.org/standard/81230.html) — AI management-system accountability, policies, objectives, controls, documented information, management review, and continual improvement.

A framework reference does not prove implementation or conformance. Alignment with these references does not by itself establish certification, legal compliance, or complete risk coverage.

# Cross-capability dependencies

## Decision Integrity

Decision Integrity supplies the decision lineage needed to assess accountability, including:

- decisions to introduce, procure, approve, restrict, change, suspend, or retire AI;

- intended purpose, permitted use, and operating boundaries;

- rationale, authority, evidence, assumptions, limitations, and unresolved issues;

- decisions to rely on, reject, challenge, override, or correct an AI output;

- decisions concerning models, prompts, configurations, data, controls, suppliers, and reliance conditions;

- consequential actions and outcomes.

Where a consequential decision leads to authorized action, an actual resulting state, verification or retest, and later reassessment, Decision Integrity preserves that lineage and status. Human–AI Accountability does not duplicate that trace; it makes explicit who is responsible and authorized to review or challenge the resulting state, who may accept continued reliance or residual risk, and who may intervene, escalate, correct, suspend, or re-authorize when reassessment is required.

Decision Integrity answers why a decision occurred, under what authority, on what evidence, and what followed. Human–AI Accountability answers who remained responsible, what oversight was required, and whether that responsibility and oversight were meaningfully exercised. Neither capability substitutes for the other.

## Organizational Memory

Organizational Memory supplies the retained context needed for accountability continuity, including:

- earlier judgments, interpretations, decisions, and exceptions;

- policies, control expectations, and their historical versions;

- known limitations, incidents, assurance findings, and unresolved issues;

- model, prompt, configuration, system, and supplier history;

- personnel and organizational transitions;

- lessons from AI-supported outcomes and interventions.

Organizational Memory preserves the changing context of accountability. Human–AI Accountability determines who must interpret, review, challenge, and act on that retained context. Retention alone does not establish accountable action.

## Governed adaptation and execution

Governed adaptation and execution is an operating outcome supported by Organizational Memory, Decision Integrity, and Human–AI Accountability; it is not a separate capability. Human–AI Accountability provides accountable ownership, authorization, review, intervention, risk acceptance, and escalation conditions when AI-enabled work, controls, responsibilities, or operating conditions change. It does not itself become live execution control or implementation authorization.

# Distributed and third-party accountability

AI-enabled work may distribute participation across executives, business owners, decision owners, reviewers, operators, subject-matter experts, risk and assurance functions, technology and data teams, developers, suppliers, model providers, outsourced operations, parent entities, subsidiaries, and affected stakeholders.

The capability must preserve:

- responsibility and authority at each material boundary;

- the business outcome owner and the party authorized to accept reliance or residual risk;

- delegated responsibilities and retained accountability;

- supplier obligations, evidence access, limitations, change notification, incident duties, and exit responsibilities;

- escalation and intervention rights across organizational boundaries;

- gaps, conflicts, and unowned responsibilities.

Participation may be distributed; accountability must not become fragmented, ambiguous, or ownerless. Supplier responsibility does not remove the using organization’s accountability for its decisions and consequences.

# Incidents, correction, and remedy

Compromised instructions, unauthorized information exposure or actions outside permitted access may require an accountable protective response. Accountable owners and authority must be explicit for containment or suspension, investigation of downstream effects, verification of corrective action, and authorization of resumption under reviewed conditions.

Where AI-supported activity causes or contributes to failure, the capability enables:

- detection and reporting of accountability failures;

- reconstruction using Decision Integrity and Organizational Memory;

- assignment of investigation and response authority;

- containment, suspension, or restriction;

- correction of decisions, outputs, records, or downstream actions;

- escalation, notification, and engagement with affected stakeholders;

- remediation and remedy where appropriate;

- reassessment of ownership, controls, evidence expectations, and authorization;

- incorporation of learning into future practice.

# Human–AI Accountability in AI-enabled transformation

Human–AI Accountability is an enabling business capability for:

- **AI Adoption & Transformation:** accountable investment, use-case, authorization, reliance, and outcome decisions;

- **AI Readiness & Enablement:** ownership of readiness gaps, evidence needs, competence, policies, and operating conditions;

- **AI Agents & Intelligent Operations:** explicit authority, boundaries, supervision, intervention, escalation, and continuity across agentic workflows;

- **AI Governance, Safety & Resilience:** accountable control ownership, assurance evidence, incident response, third-party risk, management review, and continual improvement.

It makes transformation governable, reviewable, defensible, and correctable. It is not merely a governance control applied after deployment.

Human–AI Accountability is a cross-cutting ShiftBy capability, not a standalone commercial service. Commercial services may draw on it at different levels of emphasis, but no service owns the capability.

## Expected outcomes

Expected outcomes include responsible AI adoption and scaling; improved decision quality and organizational trust; stronger resilience, assurance, and continuity; reduced risk of unowned or unchallengeable activity; meaningful oversight and clearer intervention, correction, escalation, and remedy; evidence-backed accountability across the AI lifecycle; explainable and testable policy and framework alignment; better learning from AI-supported work; and accountability visible across organizational and supplier boundaries.

# Capability boundaries and limitations

Human–AI Accountability is not itself:

- a complete Responsible AI framework;

- legal or regulatory interpretation;

- a certification or compliance-management system;

- an audit-log or provenance repository;

- model explainability or monitoring alone;

- cybersecurity, privacy, risk, records, or knowledge management;

- Decision Integrity, Organizational Memory, or governed adaptation and execution;

- a technical product, workflow, database, AI model, or agent.

It connects these disciplines, controls, mechanisms, and capabilities around explicit organizational responsibility and authority without absorbing them.

Accountability may not be reliably established where evidence is incomplete, ownership is unclear, accounts conflict, suppliers or external systems are opaque, models or prompts change without control, outputs are unreviewed, automation is inappropriate, authority is unclear, intervention is insufficient, affected stakeholders cannot seek correction, legitimate privacy or security constraints limit evidence, or organizational resistance prevents responsible practice. Retrospective reconstruction must not be represented as equivalent to contemporaneous evidence.

# Audiences, questions, and decisions

## Audiences

Human–AI Accountability is relevant to business owners, accountable executives, decision owners, approvers, operators, developers, model providers, suppliers, reviewers, risk and compliance functions, legal and privacy teams, cybersecurity and safety teams, auditors, affected stakeholders, and personnel responsible for intervention, correction, escalation, or remedy.

## Questions it helps answer

- Who is responsible for the AI-enabled activity, decision, outcome, and remedy?

- Who has authority to approve use, challenge reliance, intervene, suspend, or retire the system?

- Was human oversight meaningful given the available competence, information, time, and authority?

- What evidence shows what the AI system did, what people did, and what remained uncertain?

- Which supplier, system, policy, control, or lifecycle boundary affects accountability?

- Who can correct the record, address harm, escalate the issue, or provide remedy?

- Has a change altered responsibility, authority, risk, or the ability to intervene?

## Decisions supported

Human–AI Accountability supports people in deciding whether to authorize, continue, restrict, pause, suspend, correct, escalate, remediate, re-approve, or retire an AI-enabled activity. It provides accountability conditions and evidence for those decisions; it does not make the decisions or replace authorized owners.

## Typical situations

Typical situations include introducing AI into consequential workflows; distributed work across teams, suppliers, providers, operators, and affected stakeholders; reliance on uncertain output; material changes to models, prompts, data sources, suppliers, configurations, or workflows; harm, failure, or disputed outcomes; and nominal human presence without sufficient information, competence, time, authority, or intervention power.
